# Interview questions that get the truth

Based on the principles in Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*: talk about their life, not
your idea; ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future; listen more
than you talk.

## Bad question, better question

| Avoid | Why | Ask instead |
|-------|-----|-------------|
| Would you use a feature that explains why a code failed? | Predicting the future; people say yes to be nice | Tell me about the last time a discount code didn't work for you. What did you do next? |
| Do you think this is a problem? | Leading; invites agreement | How do you handle that today? |
| How much would you pay for this? | Hypothetical | What have you spent (time or money) trying to solve this? |
| Don't you hate it when...? | Leading | Walk me through what happened. |
| Is this design easy to use? | Opinion | (Watch them do a task. Count where they hesitate.) |
| What features do you want? | Users design poorly in the abstract | What were you trying to get done when you got stuck? |
| Why didn't you finish your order? | "Why" makes people invent tidy reasons | What was going on when you stopped? |

## Interview structure (30 to 45 minutes)

1. **Warm-up (3 min)**: their role or context, how often they do the activity.
2. **The last time (15 min)**: "Tell me about the last time you..." Get the full story in
   order. Probe for specifics: when, where, what they saw, what they did, who else was involved.
3. **Workarounds (10 min)**: what they do today, what it costs them, what they have tried.
4. **Importance (5 min)**: how often it happens, what happens if it is never fixed.
5. **Wrap-up (2 min)**: "What else should I have asked?" and who else to talk to.

## Probes that keep the story going

- "What happened next?"
- "Can you show me?" (screen share beats memory)
- "You said it was annoying. What did you actually do?"
- "How did you know that?"
- "When was the last time that happened?"

## Signals to write down

- **Strong**: they already spent time, money, or effort on a workaround; it happened
  recently and often; they raised it before you asked.
- **Weak**: compliments, "that would be cool", general statements ("I always..."),
  future promises ("I would definitely...").

## Usability test tasks

Write tasks as goals with realistic context, never as UI instructions:

- Good: "You got an email with the code SAVE10. Buy the blue mug using it."
- Bad: "Click 'Add promo code' and type SAVE10."

Ask them to think aloud. Do not help. Note where they hesitate, backtrack, or say "hmm".
